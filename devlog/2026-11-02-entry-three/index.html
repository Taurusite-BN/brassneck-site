This site has said November since the summer. It is November, the game is two chapters into six, and it is not coming out this month. The new date and the arithmetic behind it are immediately below, because I said any change would be announced here rather than quietly edited. After that: chapter two went to a tester who took it apart with great precision and no mercy, and the most useful thing in his notes was a fault I had already found, already fixed once, written a rule about, and then committed again four weeks later in the chapter I wrote after writing the rule.

November was the date. It is November.

The delay, said plainly

The game is not coming out this month and it was never going to be close. The new date is April 2027.

I am not going to dress that up, so here is the actual arithmetic instead. There are six chapters. Two of them exist. The observed pace is roughly one chapter a month — and that is for the two shortest chapters in the game, with chapter four still ahead and already identified as carrying more writing than any other scene in the project. There is also an audio pass that has not been started, because a game about confinement and tension currently makes no sound whatsoever.

Four chapters, one audio pass, five months. That is the sum. It is tight and it is honest, and it assumes the remaining chapters get built the way chapter two was — spine first at full quality, the optional content lean, filled out after it has been tested rather than before.

Why the date was wrong, which is not the same as the game being late

I did not miss November because of one bad month. I set the date before chapter one had been through a single playtest.

Three rounds of testing have happened since. They found a prose fault running through the whole script, an entire chapter's worth of contradictions, a time economy that punishes curiosity, and a hidden system whose feedback reads as the game contradicting itself. None of that was in the estimate, because you cannot budget for what testing is going to tell you — that is the entire point of testing. Every one of those findings has made the game better and every one of them cost weeks.

So the date was a guess made at the least informed moment in the project, and it was wrong in the direction guesses usually are.

What would move it again

Worth naming now rather than in March. Audio is the one genuine unknown. Everything else left on the list is work of a kind I have already done once and can estimate. Audio has never been started, has no plan behind it, and has to be in before strangers play the game — a silent build tests a different game than the one being made. If April slips, that is where I would put money.

And the one thing I can say without hedging: nobody has paid for anything. There are no pre-orders, no early access, no store page. The only thing a late game costs you at this point is the waiting, and I would rather be straight about the length of it than quietly move a number on a web page and hope nobody had it written down.

The rule I broke four weeks after writing it

Playtest three · the one that stung

Back in August a tester told me the prose was over-explaining — a sentence lands an image, and then a second clause trots along behind it explaining what the image was for. Sixty-nine lines got rewritten. I wrote the diagnosis down as a rule: a trailing clause survives only if it hands the player a fact they cannot get anywhere else. Filed, dated, done.

Then I wrote chapter two, and this round's notes came back with, among other things:

the noteWe've returned to "the way something something".

Reader, we had. The specific tic is the hollow simile — the comparison that compares a thing to itself and tells you nothing. "The deck wakes you the way it always does." "Canvas over wood, the way they all are." It sounds like observation. It is grammatically a simile and semantically a shrug.

Here is the part worth writing down, because it is not really about prose:

A rule nobody can run gets forgotten. I had turned a real finding into a document, and a document is a thing you agree with and then stop reading. The fix was not more discipline. It was to make the rule fail the build.

So the linter reads my writing now

Three new checks, and the third is the one I would steal if I were you:

  • Hollow similes fail. Not "the way" — that is a perfectly good construction and the voice needs it. Specifically the self-referential form. Nine earned similes sit on an allow-list with a one-line note each explaining why that one is doing a job.
  • Density is budgeted. More than three of them in a single file and the check fails regardless of whether each one is defensible. Individually fine, collectively a tic.
  • The known offenders are baselined, and the baseline only shrinks. The five I already knew about are listed so the build stays green while the rewrites are reviewed — and the list is allowed to get shorter and never longer. That is the whole trick. A ratchet costs nothing and it means the fault can only ever be going away.
$ python3 check.py checked 22 files · 74 labels · 18 screens · 102 images PROSE WATCH chapter2.rpy line 51 hollow simile: "the way it always does" PROSE WATCH chapter2.rpy line 84 trailing explainer clause

There is also a softer advisory pass for trailing explainers and sentences with four commas in them. That one does not fail the build, because this game's voice is long and rhythmic on purpose and I am not going to let a script bully it into staccato. Advisory is the right strength for a matter of taste. Failing is the right strength for a tic.

Re-read, but never retaken

Two correct and opposite requests

In August I turned the scroll-back off. Not to be difficult: scroll-back in this engine lets you rewind the game state, and that meant you could undo the central decision in the game — committing a length of thread you cannot get back — and simply have the thread again. The only economy the game has, dismantled by a mouse wheel.

This round the tester wanted it back, because he was cross-examining the script and could not re-read the line he had just clicked past. Also correct. A tester who cannot look at the previous sentence is being asked to review from memory.

Both requests are right and they sound mutually exclusive. They are not:

Scroll-back is on, and a single call blocks it at the exact moment a decision is written down. You can rewind freely through everything that was said. You cannot rewind past a choice. The rewind hits the moment you decided and stops dead there, which is also — and I did not plan this — a rather good metaphor for the entire game.

It is one line in the function that records decisions. Everything else came for free, because every choice in the game already goes through that one place.

Twenty-one of forty

The humbling one

Before touching anything, I checked every line the notes quoted against the actual source. Twenty-one of the forty were already fixed. Some of them months ago.

Which means either his build predates September, or he is keeping a cumulative list and re-sending it. Either way an evening of somebody's attention went on a version of the game that does not exist, and I did not find that out until I went looking.

The lesson is entirely mine and it is embarrassingly basic. Send dated builds. Put the version number on the title screen. Ask which one they played before reading a word of the notes. A tester whose feedback appears to be ignored stops giving you feedback, and he would have been completely right to conclude that, because from where he was sitting that is exactly what it looked like.

One scene, two notes, one bug

And the other one was the game working

Two of the reported contradictions came from the same few minutes of play, and they turned out to be opposites.

The game has a hidden exposure value. Do your trade in the open and it goes down; be somewhere you have no reason to be and it goes up. The cook's dialogue reads off it: at low exposure he has nothing to say to you, and higher up he starts repeating your own movements back to you as gossip.

The note was that the cook contradicts the premise — the game says nobody watches you, and then a man tells you he knows where you have been. But that was the system working. He said it because the player had been seen, which is precisely what the mechanic is for.

The real fault is one layer down: the value is invisible, so its feedback reads as the game contradicting itself rather than as consequence. That is not a dialogue problem and it cannot be fixed in dialogue. It wants the one-line atmosphere reading that has been specced since the summer and is still unbuilt — a single sentence on the ship's plan telling you how the ship is currently looking at you. Still unbuilt. Now clearly load-bearing.

And the other note from the same scene: the bosun really was a bug. His "I am told you have been busy" fired unconditionally, including at zero exposure, where it is simply false. One missing condition. Same scene, two complaints, one of them a mechanic and one of them me not gating a line.

Four keys or six

Nobody reported this, which is the interesting part

While cross-checking the notes I found a divergence nobody had noticed, including me.

There is a ring of keys that matters a great deal, and the number of them is a clue. The design documents say six, with a seventh missing. The code says four, with a fifth missing — consistently, in nine separate places, because at some point the art came back with four keys painted on it and I changed the writing to match the picture and never went back to the documents.

No tester will ever catch it, because the game is internally consistent. Only the canon is wrong. Which is its own small lesson about documentation: the thing most likely to be out of date is the thing nobody has to run.

The trailer is not going to be a trailer

A decision made early so it cannot be made badly later

Trailer work is months away, but I wanted the approach settled before anybody is tired and in a hurry, because that is when you make this particular mistake.

This is a static-image game. A trailer full of swooping cinematic motion would misrepresent it even if every frame were beautiful — and the people who wishlist on that basis are precisely the people who leave the refund reviews. Steam's own rules are the same point in colder language: the store page has to represent the product.

So the plan is animated stills, type cards and sound. Slow push-ins on paintings that already exist, cuts that hold a face completely still for two full seconds, and no score at all until the last fifteen seconds. Which is, as it happens, simply how the good trailers in this genre are made.

The most important shot in it is screen-recorded from the running game: the moment you have opened something, taken what was inside, and the two buttons appear — close it quickly, or close it properly — while the footsteps overhead keep counting. Six seconds, and it is the only proof in the whole piece that this is a game rather than a book. That goes three-quarters of the way through, where trailers put their hook.
